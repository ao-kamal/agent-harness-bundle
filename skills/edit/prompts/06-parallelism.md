### Parallelism framework explanation
Parallelism means elements must be in the same form.

Here's a famous example:

"Give a person a fish and you feed them for a day; teach a person to fish and you feed them for a lifetime."

Do you feel the balance? It's an intentional pattern. Parallelism is the unsung hero of eloquent writing.

Think of parallelism as the rhythm in music. When a song flows seamlessly, it's pleasing to the ear.

In writing, parallelism creates a rhythm that makes your text more readable and engaging. It's not just to be grammatically correct; it's to craft a reading experience that feels natural and effortless.

There are five common ways to use parallelism:

1. When elements are joined by coordinating conjunctions ("and, but, or, so, etc.")
2. In lists or series
3. In comparisons
4. When elements are joined by linking verbs ("is")
5. When elements are joined by correlative conjunctions ("either/or, neither/nor, etc.")

### Parallelism examples

**1. Conjunctions**

Parallel: I like running and "listening" to music.

Not parallel: I like running and "to listen" to music.

"To listen" is the infinitive form of "listen", whereas "running" is the gerund (it ends in "ing"). They don't match, so they aren't parallel.

Picture coordinating conjunctions like dancers in a pair. They must move in sync.

When you connect elements with "and", "but", or "or", ensure they perform the same grammatical dance. "I enjoy reading and writing" is parallel. "I enjoy reading and to write" is like one dancer doing the tango and the other the waltz.

**2. Lists or series**

Parallel:

Make sure to answer:

- Why it matters
- "How they can do it"

Not parallel:

Make sure to answer:

- Why it matters
- "How will they do it?"

"How will they do it?" is in the interrogative (or question) form, whereas "Why it matters" is in the positive form. They must match.

Think of lists like best friends dressed in matching outfits. Each outfit mirrors the other in form. "She loves singing, dancing, and to play the guitar" is a mismatch. Make it "She loves singing, dancing, and playing the guitar."

**3. Comparisons**

Parallel: Trying and failing far exceeds "not trying" at all.

Not parallel: Trying and failing far exceeds "not to try" at all.

The gerund "trying" starts the sentence, so it must be followed by the gerund again in the second half to match.

Comparisons in writing should be like twins—similar in every aspect.

**4. Linking verbs**

Parallel: "To know" her is "to love" her.

Not parallel: To know her "is loving" her.

Here, the infinitive is incorrectly followed by a gerund.

Think of linking verbs as mirrors reflecting the form on either side. "His main hobbies are hiking and to fish" breaks the reflection. Instead, "His main hobbies are hiking and fishing" maintains the parallel structure.

**5. Correlative conjunctions**

Parallel: Frankie the dog is either sleeping "or zooming".

Not parallel: Frankie the dog is either sleeping "or she is zooming".

"She is" is unnecessary in this instance.

Correlative conjunctions are like bookends. They need to support the structure in a similar fashion. "Either read thoroughly or skimming" is off-balance. It should be "Either read thoroughly or skim."
